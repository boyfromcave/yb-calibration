In-term claims: who gets what, and why anyone claims

The same vault, the same YEC price path, the same rules for splitting the collateral. The only difference between the two pictures is when the claim is allowed to happen.

Both pictures use one example vault. The owner locks 2,000 YEC at $0.40 ($800 of collateral) and mints 100 YED ($100 of debt) for a 90-day term. That is an 800 % ratio at mint. The claim threshold θ is 125 %, the value the node already has (claimThresholdBps). Over the term the YEC price falls to $0.05.

Today: the claim path opens only after the term

In-term claims: the same path, the claim allowed at the threshold

Who may claim, and when

Nobody can claim before the threshold. From day 0 to day 59, while the collateral is worth more than 125 % of the debt, a claim transaction is invalid and every node rejects it: the vault is the owner's. Once collateral is at or below θ × debt, anyone may claim, by burning the full 100 YED debt, and the first valid claim wins. The owner's own exit is separate and unchanged: the owner can redeem by burning the debt at any time in the term, before or after the threshold. The open design question is only whether, once the threshold is met, the claim is open to anyone at once (what the study assumed) or offered first to a defined party such as the attestor set for a short window.

Who gets what when the day-60 claim completes

The split rule is the one the node already applies to post-term claims (RED-5): the claimant receives collateral worth θ × debt at the claim price, and everything above that goes back to the owner. The claimant never receives the whole vault.

Why anyone claims: nobody buys bad debt

The confusion in "buying the bad debt" is the key point. A claimant does not take over an underwater vault. The claim path only pays while the collateral is still worth more than the debt, and it stops paying at exactly the moment the vault would become bad debt. In the example, the claimant hands over 100 YED ($100) and receives 2,000 YEC worth $125. That $25 is the fee for watching the chain and acting. If the claimant waits, the reward shrinks toward zero as the price falls to the debt line; below it, there is no reward and no claim. So the incentive runs the right way: the earliest valid claim pays the most, which means claims happen at θ, not after.

Under today's rule the same claimant is offered the day-90 trade instead: burn $100 of YED for $50 of YEC. Nobody takes it, which is exactly why the bad debt sits there.

Why it halves the collateral requirement

The ratio exists to cover the fall in price between the last moment anyone could act and the moment the vault would be underwater. Today that is the whole term: the mint ratio must cover a 90-day worst drawdown, which is where 725 % to 1,925 % comes from. With in-term claims it is the cancel window plus a block or two: a 24-hour worst drawdown. The study measured the difference on YEC's own history as 3.75× needed today against 1.5× to 1.75× with in-term claims, at the same 2 % tolerance.

What the study could not model, and what the decision turns on

  • A claimant has to exist, holding YED, at a moment when the price is falling. The study assumes one appears at the oracle price. Is a 25 % margin enough to make people keep YED in reserve for this?
  • Selling the collateral. The claimant receives YEC and must sell it into a falling market; with today's $119–188 of bid depth, a $125 claim is fine but many at once are not. The margin θ is also the claimant's slippage budget.
  • The attestor cancel power. The cancel window exists to stop claims at a wrong price. In-term, it is also a 24-hour window in which the price can keep falling; the study's 24 h is the knob.
  • Owner protection. An owner who redeems before θ loses nothing; an owner who does not is closed out at θ with the residual returned. That is a change in the product promise ("locked for the term") and should be disclosed.

What it changes in the node: the YED module's claim rule (RED-4) gains the in-term condition; the vault template's APP branch opens from the mint height instead of the term end; the primitive, the attestor set and the cancel mechanism are unchanged. A consensus change on the upgrade line, under the review gate, and a design decision before any of that: θ, the cancel delay, and who may claim.
